# United Kingdom government frameworks and guidance for evaluating AI systems (status as of November 2026)

## What evaluation frameworks and tools has the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) published?

### Takeaway
AISI (renamed from "AI Safety Institute" to "AI Security Institute" on 14 February 2025) has produced the most concrete government-built evaluation artifacts anywhere: the open-source Inspect evaluation framework (now co-developed with Meridian Labs), a published approach-to-evaluations methodology, named pre-deployment evaluations of specific frontier models (jointly with the US institute), participation in the International Network of AI Safety Institutes' joint testing exercises, a public research agenda, and — since December 2025 — a flagship Frontier AI Trends Report synthesizing two years of government-led testing.

### Cited Findings

**Inspect (evaluation framework/tool)**
- Inspect is "a framework for frontier AI evaluations developed by the UK AI Security Institute and Meridian Labs" — an open-source Python framework for LLM evaluations covering coding, agentic tasks, reasoning, knowledge, behavior, and multimodal understanding, installed via `pip install inspect-ai` — [Inspect documentation](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/)
- Core architecture: **datasets** (labeled samples with `input`/`target` fields, loadable from Hugging Face, CSV, JSON, or memory via `FieldSpec` mapping), **solvers** (from a single `generate()` call up to full tool-using agents), and **scorers** (text comparison such as `includes()`, model grading such as `model_graded_qa()`, or custom schemes) — [Inspect documentation](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/)
- Agent support: a built-in `react()` reason-act-observe agent, multi-agent primitives, and the ability to run external agents (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI are named); built-in tools include bash, Python, text editing, web search, computer use, and MCP tools — [Inspect documentation](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/)
- Sandboxing: untrusted model-generated code runs in isolated environments — Docker, Kubernetes, Modal, Proxmox, Vagrant — with other systems addable via an extension API (e.g., CTF-style tasks use `sandbox="docker"`) — [Inspect documentation](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/)
- Ships with 200+ prebuilt evaluations, a web-based log viewer ("Inspect View"), and a VS Code extension; source code at [github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_ai](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_ai) (MIT-licensed per the PyPI/GitHub metadata) — [Inspect documentation](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/); [PyPI inspect-ai](https://pypi.org/project/inspect-ai/)

**Approach to evaluations**
- "AI Safety Institute: approach to evaluations", published 9 February 2024 by DSIT/AISI, sets out AISI's evaluation techniques, an initial research agenda, criteria for selecting models to evaluate, and three case studies from the AI Safety Summit covering misuse, autonomous systems, and societal impacts; it states AISI had begun pre-deployment testing for "potentially harmful capabilities" and names evaluations, foundational AI research, and information exchange as AISI's three core functions — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-safety-institute-approach-to-evaluations). Note: this is an early-2024 document carrying the institute's pre-rename title; treat as historical/foundational rather than the current operational description.
- "Early lessons from evaluating frontier AI systems" (AISI, 24 October 2024) is the richest public statement of AISI's evaluation methodology. Key points: independent evaluations are **not** a safety "certification" (the science is "too nascent") but serve to verify company claims, improve safety, advance evaluation science, and inform government preparedness; evaluations should be *predictive* (partial scoring and sub-task milestones to gauge distance from dangerous thresholds); risk modelling should drive which evaluations get built; elicitation matters (tasks used for both elicitation and testing inflate results, so AISI separates them); quality assurance relies on **preregistration** (methods and report drafts documented and approved before model access); effective testing requires "Helpful Only" model versions, sufficient testing windows, and information security safeguards; and AISI describes a tiered testing model (light automated / standard with manual work / elevated with extensive red teaming) as work in progress — [AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/early-lessons-from-evaluating-frontier-ai-systems)
- By October 2024 AISI reported results from testing 4 LLMs from major companies across misuse (chemical, biological, cyber), societal impacts, autonomous systems, and safeguards, using automated capability evaluations, AI agent evaluations (including Capture the Flag challenges), expert red teaming, and human uplift studies — [AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/early-lessons-from-evaluating-frontier-ai-systems)

**Pre-deployment testing of frontier models**
- UK AISI and US AISI conducted a joint pre-deployment evaluation of Anthropic's upgraded Claude 3.5 Sonnet (released 22 October 2024); UK AISI tested safeguards with public and privately developed jailbreaks and found the tested version's safeguards "can be routinely circumvented" — [AISI](https://aisi.gov.uk/work/pre-deployment-evaluation-of-anthropics-upgraded-claude-3-5-sonnet)
- A second joint UK–US pre-deployment evaluation covered OpenAI's o1 model (released 5 December 2024), with separate but complementary tests across cyber capabilities, biological capabilities, and software/AI development; the institutes operate under a Memorandum of Understanding — [AISI](https://aisi.gov.uk/work/pre-deployment-evaluation-of-openais-o1-model)
- AISI frames pre-deployment testing as running "alongside our post-deployment testing to monitor performance 'in the wild' and create a baseline to compare new systems", and notes "model evaluations are a new and rapidly evolving science, and we routinely iterate on our approach" — [AISI](https://aisi.gov.uk/work/pre-deployment-evaluation-of-openais-o1-model)

**Frontier AI Trends Report (December 2025)**
- AISI published its first Frontier AI Trends Report on 18 December 2025 — its "first public overview of how the most advanced AI systems are evolving", drawing on two years of government-led testing; it covers trends in cyber security, biology and chemistry assistance, autonomous behaviour, safeguards, and human influence — [GOV.UK factsheet](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet); report landing page at [aisi.gov.uk/frontier-ai-trends-report](https://aisi.gov.uk/frontier-ai-trends-report)
- The factsheet describes AISI's operating model: continuous evaluation (independent testing of leading systems before and after deployment to find safeguard vulnerabilities), developer collaboration (test findings fed back to companies to strengthen safeguards, including on cyber tasks and agent behaviour), national security coordination (with the National Cyber Security Centre and defence science agencies), and targeted research funding (e.g., the Alignment Project on risks such as unintended autonomous behaviour) — [GOV.UK factsheet](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet)
- Secondary coverage states the report draws on testing of more than 30 frontier systems since November 2023 and is intended as the first in a series — [techUK](https://www.techuk.org/resource/uk-ai-security-institute-releases-inaugural-frontier-ai-trends-report.html); the model count is **not** confirmed in the GOV.UK factsheet itself.

**Joint testing exercises (International Network of AI Safety Institutes)**
- The Network ran an initial pilot testing exercise of a foundation model as a proof of concept ahead of its November 2024 San Francisco convening; the first full exercise tested Meta's Llama 3.1 405B on general knowledge, multilingual capabilities, and closed-domain hallucinations, led by technical experts from US AISI, UK AISI, and Singapore AISI — [Singapore AISI](https://sgaisi.sg/resources/a-pilot-testing-exercise-from-the-international-network-of-ai-safety-institutes/)
- The second joint exercise (published around the February 2025 Paris AI Action Summit) focused on improving methodologies for model evaluations across global languages, led by Singapore, Japan, and the UK — [Japan AISI report PDF](https://aisi.go.jp/assets/pdf/International-Network-of-AI-Safety-Institutes-Joint-Testing-Exercise-Improving-Methodologies-for-AI-Model-Evaluations-Across-Global-Languages.pdf)
- A third joint testing exercise, published July 2025 to accompany the Network's Vancouver convening, covered agentic testing, including leakage of sensitive information and fraud/cybersecurity — [European Commission AI Office](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-office-contributes-third-joint-testing-exercise-international-network-ai-safety-institutes)

**Research agenda**
- AISI maintains a public research agenda page splitting its risk research into domain-specific work (e.g., cyber misuse, dual-use science) and generalised work on measurement methods that apply across domains; one stated focus is how frontier AI could enable malicious cyber activity such as attacks on critical national infrastructure, and how developers could mitigate it — [AISI research agenda](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/research-agenda) (page content partially obscured in retrieval; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- AISI's publications collectively form a de facto methodology curriculum for frontier-model evaluation: Inspect supplies the tooling layer; "Early lessons" supplies the methodological principles (elicitation, preregistration, tiered testing, predictive evaluations); the pre-deployment reports supply worked examples; the Trends Report supplies longitudinal synthesis. For an educational Atlas, "Early lessons" is probably the single most teaching-rich UK document on how government evaluation actually works.
- The addition of Meridian Labs as Inspect co-developer (Meridian was co-founded in 2025 by Inspect's main author JJ Allaire) indicates Inspect's maintenance has partially moved outside government while remaining the official AISI framework — worth tracking for the Atlas's tool entries.
- The shift in emphasis from per-model pre-deployment reports (2024) to an aggregate annual Trends Report (December 2025) suggests AISI now treats individual model evaluations as routine/confidential inputs and publishes synthesized trends instead.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full text of the AISI research agenda page (aisi.gov.uk pages returned JavaScript-blocked content in cached text), so its exact structure and date are unverified beyond the summary above.
- I found no public AISI pre-deployment evaluation reports for individual models newer than o1 (December 2024); whether later per-model reports exist non-publicly or were folded into the Trends Report is unconfirmed.
- The specific findings of the Frontier AI Trends Report (per-domain results) were not retrieved; the GOV.UK factsheet deliberately omits them. The full report at aisi.gov.uk would need direct reading.
- A secondary source (casrai.org, citing a claimed June 2026 AISI post) asserts METR and Apollo Research adopted Inspect; I could not verify this against a primary source, so it should not be used.
- The Inspect license is reported as MIT in package metadata but the docs page itself only links to the repo LICENSE file; verify at the GitHub repo if the Atlas states a license.

## What AI assurance guidance has DSIT published?

### Takeaway
DSIT has built a layered AI assurance programme: foundational concepts (Introduction to AI assurance, February 2024), a worked-example library defining eight assurance technique types (Portfolio of AI assurance techniques, June 2023), a market analysis (Assuring a Responsible Future for AI, November 2024), an organisational self-assessment baseline (AI Management Essentials, consulted 2024–25 with government response February 2026), and a market-building plan for independent assurers (Trusted third-party AI assurance roadmap, September 2025) — all tracing back to the CDEI's 2021 assurance-ecosystem roadmap.

### Cited Findings

**Introduction to AI assurance (February 2024)**
- Published 12 February 2024 by DSIT as practitioner guidance (HTML + 44-page PDF); an introduction to how assurance techniques support responsible AI, explaining key assurance concepts/terms, situating them in the wider AI governance landscape, and giving an overview of assurance mechanisms and global technical standards; it supports the March 2023 AI regulation white paper's five cross-cutting principles and states it "will be regularly updated"; listed under the Responsible AI Toolkit collection — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/introduction-to-ai-assurance)

**Portfolio of AI assurance techniques (June 2023, ongoing)**
- Published 7 June 2023 by DSIT (developed by its Responsible Technology Adoption Unit, initially with techUK): a searchable library of real-world case studies of AI assurance techniques, each mapped to the white paper's cross-sector regulatory principles; inclusion is not government endorsement — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/portfolio-of-ai-assurance-techniques)
- The Portfolio defines **eight assurance technique types** — impact assessment, impact evaluation, bias audit, compliance audit, certification, conformity assessment, performance testing, and formal verification — and includes a lifecycle table mapping techniques to stages from scoping through retirement — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/portfolio-of-ai-assurance-techniques)

**Lineage: 2021 roadmap and 2024 market report**
- "The roadmap to an effective AI assurance ecosystem" (CDEI, published 8 December 2021, as an independent report on GOV.UK) was "the first publication of its kind", setting out steps to grow "a mature, world-class AI assurance industry" within about five years, across six priority areas including generating demand and supporting the assurance market — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-roadmap-to-an-effective-ai-assurance-ecosystem/the-roadmap-to-an-effective-ai-assurance-ecosystem). Status: superseded in practice by the 2024–2025 publications below, though not formally withdrawn (verify withdrawal status on the page itself).
- "Assuring a responsible future for AI" (DSIT research and analysis, published 6 November 2024) surveys the state of the UK AI assurance market, identifies growth opportunities, and sets out planned government action — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/assuring-a-responsible-future-for-ai); secondary reporting attributes to the launch a claimed market-growth potential of £6.5bn — [techUK](https://www.techuk.org/resource/dsit-secretary-of-state-announces-rta-ai-assurance-initiative-6-5bn-market-growth-potential-and-new-public-consultation.html)

**AI Management Essentials (AIME)**
- AIME is a DSIT self-assessment tool helping organisations (especially SMEs/start-ups) assess and implement responsible AI management systems and processes; it assesses organisational management practices, **not** AI products or services; consultation ran 6 November 2024 – 29 January 2025 and received 65 responses — [GOV.UK consultation page](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/ai-management-essentials-tool)
- The consultation materials comprise a guidance document ([Guidance for using the AI Management Essentials tool](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/ai-management-essentials-tool/guidance-for-using-the-ai-management-essentials-tool)) and a self-assessment PDF — [AIME self-assessment PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672a5706094e4e60c466d19f/AI_Management_Essentials_tool_Self-Assessment.pdf)
- The government response was published with the consultation outcome; the GOV.UK consultation page shows its last update as 6 February 2026, and the response covers respondents' views on the tool's general impression, structure/format, assessment questions, and proportionality/procurement — [GOV.UK consultation outcome](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/ai-management-essentials-tool/outcome/guidance-for-using-the-ai-management-essentials-tool-government-response). (One search summary dated the response December 2025 with a 6 February 2026 update; the page itself shows 6 February 2026 — use the page date.)
- Secondary analyses consistently describe AIME as distilling principles from ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and the EU AI Act — [IoD explainer](https://www.iod.com/resources/science-innovation-and-tech/business-explainer-the-ai-management-essentials-tool/); the GOV.UK page retrieved did not itself name these standards (verify in the guidance PDF before stating as fact).

**Trusted third-party AI assurance roadmap (September 2025)**
- Published 3 September 2025 by DSIT; sets out the plan to grow a credible third-party AI assurance market, delivering on commitment 29 of the AI Opportunities Action Plan; describes assurance as providing "ways to measure, evaluate and communicate the trustworthiness of AI systems" — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap)
- Names four market barriers: unclear **quality** of assurance services (immature technical standards and quality infrastructure); **skills** shortages with undefined competencies; **information access** (assurers often cannot get enough system information, due to confidentiality/security concerns); and limited **innovation** forums for collaborative R&D — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap)
- Considers three quality-assurance models for the assurance market itself: **professionalisation** (individual certification/registration — strongest near-term option), **process certification** (quality marks for specific processes such as bias audits — market judged not yet mature enough), and **accreditation** of firms via UKAS (accredited management-system certification seen as most feasible near-term route, but relevant standards are limited) — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap)
- Concrete commitments: a stakeholder consortium to develop a skills and competencies framework (to underpin a future professional certification scheme); mapping the information different assurance services require (covering requirements, inputs/outputs, algorithms and parameters, oversight mechanisms, governance documentation) with proposed interventions including secure technical access environments and information-access standards; and an **AI Assurance Innovation Fund of £11 million**, first round opening Spring 2026 — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap)
- Follow-through: techUK was appointed to lead the industry advisory group of the AI Assurance Consortium — [techUK](https://www.techuk.org/resource/dsit-s-ai-assurance-roadmap-takes-its-next-step-techuk-to-lead-the-industry-advisory-group-of-ai-assurance-consortium.html)

### Inferences
- The Portfolio's eight technique types (impact assessment → formal verification) are the closest thing the UK has to an official taxonomy of AI evaluation/assurance methods, and map naturally onto an Atlas page distinguishing organisational assurance (AIME), system-level techniques (Portfolio), and market infrastructure (third-party roadmap).
- The UK's model is explicitly market-based rather than regulatory: none of these documents *requires* evaluation of private-sector AI; they define, catalogue, and professionalise voluntary assurance. This contrasts usefully with the EU AI Act's mandatory conformity assessment and is a teachable distinction.
- AIME remained in "consultation outcome" form as of the February 2026 response; whether a final standalone AIME tool (e.g., integrated into government procurement, as the consultation contemplated) has shipped by November 2026 was not confirmed and should be labelled accordingly.

### Gaps
- The retrieved Introduction to AI assurance page did not enumerate the specific assurance mechanisms covered in its 44-page PDF; the Portfolio page's eight technique types are the verified taxonomy. (A February 2025 "AI assurance: introduction and guidance for industry" update was not found/checked — unconfirmed whether the Feb 2024 guide was updated.)
- Final AIME tool status after the February 2026 government response (digital tool? procurement integration?) is unverified.
- Whether the first £11m Innovation Fund round actually opened in Spring 2026, and any outputs of the AI Assurance Consortium's skills framework, were not verified.
- The £6.5bn market-growth figure comes from techUK's report of a ministerial announcement, not from a retrieved GOV.UK page.

## What does the UK government require for AI used in the public sector?

### Takeaway
Two instruments dominate: the AI Playbook for the UK Government (February 2025, GDS) — guidance with ten principles for safe AI use by civil servants, successor to the Generative AI Framework for HMG — and the Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard (ATRS), which is mandatory for government departments and public-facing arm's-length bodies and produces published records about algorithmic tools, making it the UK's main public-sector AI transparency/accountability mechanism.

### Cited Findings

**AI Playbook for the UK Government**
- Published 10 February 2025 by the Government Digital Service as guidance for civil servants and government organisations on "using AI safely, effectively and securely"; 118-page PDF (ISBN 9781036688745) plus HTML and posters; it expands on the Generative AI Framework for HMG (January 2024) and was retitled to cover a wider range of AI technologies; includes 10 principles for civil servants to uphold and guidance on selecting, buying, and deploying AI in government — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government)
- The GOV.UK landing page does not itself enumerate the 10 principles or its testing/assurance content; those are inside the full document (see Gaps) — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government)

**Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard (ATRS)**
- The ATRS is "a standardised way for public sector organisations to publish information" about how and why they use algorithmic tools; the hub collection was published 5 January 2023 and last updated 8 May 2025; the teams that developed it now sit in GDS; development involved cross-sector stakeholders, a public engagement study, and pilots — [GOV.UK ATRS hub](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub)
- Mandatory for all government departments and for arm's-length bodies that deliver public or frontline services or directly interact with the public; a scope and exemptions policy (December 2024) details coverage; the Data Standards Authority recommends it for the wider public sector — [GOV.UK ATRS hub](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub)
- Completed records are published in a public repository with a search page (added March 2024) — [GOV.UK algorithmic transparency records](https://www.gov.uk/algorithmic-transparency-records)
- A new template version was published March 2024, with template and guidance most recently updated 8 May 2025; the January 2025 Blueprint for Modern Digital Government commits to building on the ATRS to increase openness about AI usage — [GOV.UK ATRS hub](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub)
- A December 2025 press release announced new published records describing how AI helps government decisions (trade, correspondence response times), alongside clarified scope for which tools records must cover — [GOV.UK press release](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-records-detail-how-ai-helps-government-make-quick-accurate-decisions-to-boost-trade-speed-up-responses-and-more)

### Inferences
- The ATRS is evaluation-relevant primarily as an *evidence-publication* requirement: it forces departments to document algorithmic tools publicly, which in practice surfaces information about purpose, oversight, and risk. For the Atlas it belongs under "transparency/documentation standards" rather than "testing frameworks".
- The Playbook is guidance, not statute; the ATRS mandate is a policy requirement for central government, not legislation. The UK has no general public-sector AI audit mandate beyond ATRS publication (consistent with third-party commentary noting the absence of a central AI register or audit mandate).

### Gaps
- The ATRS hub page does not describe the record template's fields (e.g., whether/how records capture testing, performance metrics, risk and impact assessments); the template and guidance documents linked from the hub would need direct reading before the Atlas describes record contents. Do not state field-level claims without that check.
- The Playbook's 10 principles and its specific content on testing, red-teaming, monitoring, or assurance could not be verified from the landing page; read the 118-page PDF/HTML before summarising them. (Secondary sources list principles like "You know what AI is and what its limitations are", but these were not verified against the primary document in this research.)

## Sector regulator guidance with concrete evaluation content (ICO, MHRA)

### Takeaway
The ICO's Guidance on AI and data protection (last updated 15 March 2023, currently flagged "under review" due to the Data (Use and Access) Act) contains the UK's most concrete regulator guidance on measuring statistical accuracy and fairness of AI systems, with an accompanying risk toolkit; the MHRA's AI Airlock (launched Spring 2024) is a regulatory sandbox that generates real evaluation evidence for AI medical devices, with a Phase 2 programme report published June 2026.

### Cited Findings

**ICO (Information Commissioner's Office)**
- The ICO's "Guidance on AI and data protection" is organised around the data protection principles; last updated 15 March 2023; it carries the notice "Due to changes made by the Data (Use and Access) Act, this guidance is under review and may be subject to change" — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/)
- Evaluation-relevant content: a dedicated chapter on **statistical accuracy** under the accuracy principle ("continues to remain key for fairness"); chapters on algorithmic fairness covering bias and discrimination, evaluating fairness and its trade-offs, processing personal data for bias mitigation, and technical bias-reduction approaches; Annex A on fairness across the AI lifecycle (sources of bias and mitigations); DPIA content in the accountability chapter; and Article 22 safeguards for solely automated decision-making — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/)
- An "AI and data protection risk toolkit" exists as a chapter under the same guidance path — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/) (toolkit contents not retrieved)
- Lineage: the guidance originated as the draft "AI auditing framework" consulted on in February–April 2020, which had two parts — auditing tools/procedures the ICO itself uses in audits and investigations, and guidance for organisations; the ICO stated a DPIA is legally required in the majority of cases where AI processes personal data — [Hunton (consultation summary)](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/ico-consultation-on-draft-ai-auditing-framework-guidance-for-organizations). The 2020 "auditing framework" label is historical; the current artifact is the guidance + toolkit above.

**MHRA (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency)**
- AI as a Medical Device (AIaMD) is regulated as a subset of software as a medical device under the Medical Devices Regulations 2002; the MHRA's **AI Airlock**, launched Spring 2024, is its first regulatory sandbox for AIaMD — a time-boxed, supervised environment where manufacturers run controlled evaluations of novel AI medical devices in a real NHS context; the first cohort had four to five projects — [GOV.UK press release (pilot)](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/mhra-trials-five-innovative-ai-technologies-as-part-of-pilot-scheme-to-change-regulatory-approach)
- The programme was expanded with additional funding for a second round (applications 23 June – 14 July 2025) — [GOV.UK press release (expansion)](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ai-breakthroughs-drive-expansion-of-airlock-testing-programme-to-support-ai-powered-healthcare-innovation)
- The AI Airlock Phase 2 Programme Report (per search metadata: issued 9 June 2026, updated 27 July 2026; explicitly not formal MHRA guidance) covers intended-purpose scope and validation, performance evaluation for AI-powered in vitro diagnostics, and predetermined change control plans with post-market surveillance; findings include that AI medical devices need a lifecycle approach with post-market monitoring, that adequate human oversight changes over a device's lifecycle and "should not be treated as a fixed safety control", and that LLM-based devices "may begin to exhibit functions beyond their intended purpose when they lack active guardrails" — [AI Airlock Phase 2 Programme Report v2 (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a676af523e57bd0a0ea6852/AI_Airlock_Phase_2_Programme_Report_v2.pdf); companion [AI Airlock AI In-Vitro Diagnostics Simulation Report (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a27ccff56960b0542c0b1f5/AI_Airlock_AI_In-Vitro_Diagnostics_Simulation_Report.pdf)
- The Airlock does not replace the standard UKCA/approval route or the need to satisfy NICE and DTAC requirements — [Mills & Reeve commentary](https://mills-reeve.com/insights/blogs/life-sciences/may-2024/a-testbed-for-medical-device-ai-the-mhra-s-ai-airl) (secondary source)

### Inferences
- For the Atlas, the ICO guidance is the best UK example of a regulator translating evaluation concepts (statistical accuracy, fairness trade-offs, bias measurement) into legal-compliance terms; the AI Airlock is the best UK example of *sandbox-generated* evaluation evidence feeding regulatory learning — two distinct evaluation-governance patterns worth contrasting.
- The ICO's "under review" flag (Data (Use and Access) Act) means Atlas text should date-stamp any ICO citation and expect revisions.

### Gaps
- The contents of the ICO's AI and data protection risk toolkit were not retrieved; describe it only as existing unless read directly.
- Whether the ICO has published revised AI guidance post-Data (Use and Access) Act (i.e., between the "under review" notice and November 2026) was not determined.
- The MHRA Phase 2 report dates (9 June 2026 / 27 July 2026) come from search-result metadata about the asset PDFs, not from a fetched GOV.UK landing page; the canonical GOV.UK publication page URL for the Phase 2 report was not captured (the stable programme page is likely under gov.uk's "AI Airlock" guidance pages — verify before citing a landing page). The broader "Software and AI as a Medical Device Change Programme" page was not checked in this research.
- MHRA guidance documents beyond the Airlock (e.g., principles for good machine learning practice co-authored with FDA/Health Canada) were out of scope of retrieved results and are not covered here.
