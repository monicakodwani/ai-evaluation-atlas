# Governments race to institutionalize AI evaluation

As of November 2026, every major AI jurisdiction has built government machinery for evaluating AI systems — but that machinery splits into four distinct institutional patterns, and only two jurisdictions make evaluation legally compulsory. The EU AI Act's Article 55 is the world's first binding model-evaluation mandate on frontier developers (enforceable since 2 August 2026), and China is the only country with a mandatory pre-launch security-assessment gate for generative AI services. Everywhere else, governments act as methodology publishers (NIST's draft TEVV-Athlon framework, Japan's AISI evaluation and red-teaming guides, Singapore's AI Verify stack), as hands-on evaluators of frontier models (the US CAISI and UK AISI, which jointly test models from all five major US labs under voluntary agreements), or as transparency enforcers that require evaluation *evidence* to be published rather than tests to be run (Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment, the UK's ATRS, the G7/OECD HAIP reporting framework). For the AI Evaluation Atlas, this landscape supplies both the richest teaching materials on how evaluation is actually done — AISI's "Early lessons," the GPAI Code of Practice's Safety and Security chapter, Japan's red-teaming guide — and a crucial honesty lesson: many flagship documents remain drafts, pilots, or secondary-sourced claims. The International AI Safety Report (February 2026), the most institutionally backed consensus document in the field, concludes that pre-deployment testing does not reliably predict real-world behaviour and that no internationally agreed evaluation methodology yet exists — per secondary summaries of the report ([Global Policy Watch](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/02/international-ai-safety-report-2026-examines-ai-capabilities-risks-and-safeguards/)). A complicating note for all US entries: a September 2026 executive order rebrands federal "AI" terminology as "Super Intelligence" ("SI"), so NIST program names and URLs are in flux (EO details rest on a law-firm alert, not the Federal Register — [Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-Executive-Order-Rebrands-AI-as-Super-Intelligence-and-Signals-Potential-Federal-Legislative-Changes)).

## The US publishes methodology as drafts while CAISI tests frontier models

The United States runs a two-track system: NIST writes voluntary evaluation methodology, while the renamed AI Safety Institute actually tests frontier models. The methodological centerpiece is **TEVV-Athlon (NIST AI 200-2)**, an August 2026 **Initial Public Draft** — not a final document — that gives organizations a four-stage method (Articulate & Organize, Define & Construct, Apply & Measure, Synthesize & Interrogate) for composing customized, multi-event evaluations of AI systems from statistical ML to agentic systems ([NIST TEVV-Athlon page](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence/ai-research/tevv-athlon-framework-evaluating-ai-systems)). Its vocabulary is genuinely teachable: **Metrology Blocks** (the concepts an evaluation assesses), **Events** (activities producing evidence for each block), and a **Toolbox** of methods, with appendices cataloguing benchmarks, red-teaming methods, and field-testing techniques ([NIST AI 200-2 ipd PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.200-2.ipd.pdf)). The triathlon metaphor is explicit — test AI across multiple events to judge overall fitness for purpose — and the framework slots into the AI RMF's Map/Govern/Manage functions, operationalizing the measurement layer the RMF never specified. The comment period closed 6 October 2026 with **no final version published as of 11 November 2026**, so the Atlas must label it a draft.

**ARIA (Assessing Risks and Impacts of AI)** is NIST's complementary evaluation *program*: a sector-agnostic exercise measuring LLM-application risk in realistic contexts through three escalating levels — model testing, red teaming, and field testing with human participants ([ARIA program page](https://ai-challenges.nist.gov/aria)). Its only completed round is the **ARIA 0.1 pilot** (December 2024–January 2025): five organizations, seven applications, three scenarios (TV Spoilers, Meal Planner, Pathfinder), with results assessed through dialogue annotation, tester questionnaires, and "measurement trees"; the pilot report (NIST AI 700-2) appeared 13 November 2025 and states explicitly that ARIA is *not* a certification program ([NIST publication page](https://www.nist.gov/publications/assessing-risks-and-impacts-ai-aria-pilot-evaluation-report)). The researchers found **no post-pilot ARIA 1.0 round**, and the program appears paused or between iterations — an inference from the page's stale milestones, not an official statement. Around these two sit NIST's maintained **Dioptra** adversarial-ML testbed ([NCCoE](https://www.nccoe.nist.gov/get-involved/attend-events/nccoe-learning-series-overview-nccoes-dioptra-aiml-test-platform)), the **GenAI challenge** whose text-to-text pilot (NIST AI 700-1, June 2025) found AI-generated summaries converging on human writing while detectors remain reasonably effective ([NIST AI 700-1 PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.700-1.pdf)), and **AI RMF 1.0**, still the current version with a revision "in progress" under the July 2025 AI Action Plan ([AIRC](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/)); a claimed April 2026 "AI RMF 2.0" draft is **uncorroborated and contradicted by later checks — do not present as fact**.

The testing arm is **CAISI** — the former US AI Safety Institute, renamed Center for AI Standards and Innovation in June 2025 and again to **CAISSI** (Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence) under the September 2026 terminology EO ([NIST CAISSI page](https://www.nist.gov/caisi)). As of May 2026 it holds **voluntary pre-deployment evaluation agreements with all five major US frontier labs** — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/05/us-frontier-ai-testing-white-house-pivots-safety)) — though the arrangements carry no legal mandate and no disclosure requirement ([CSA research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research-rb/csa-research-note-nist-caisi-predeployment-evaluations-20260/)). Its published output tilts toward national-security capability assessment of PRC models: the September 2025 DeepSeek evaluation ([NIST bulletin](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USNIST/bulletins/3f48ce1)), 2026 assessments of Z.ai's GLM-5.2 and GLM-5.3, and a joint UK AISI/CAISI assessment of Kimi K3's cyber capabilities ([NIST CAISSI page](https://www.nist.gov/caisi)). A reported figure of **40+ frontier-model evaluations across seventeen Action Plan taskings is medium-confidence secondary reporting**, not verified against a NIST primary source ([FedScoop](https://fedscoop.com/?p=89166)). For federal agencies themselves, **OMB M-25-21** is the binding rule: six minimum practices for high-impact AI, led by documented pre-deployment testing — including, notably for teaching, explicit authorization of black-box behavioural testing ("querying the AI service and observing the outputs") when agencies lack model access — plus impact assessments with independent review, ongoing monitoring, and waiver machinery ([M-25-21 PDF](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf)). FDA's lifecycle guidance for AI-enabled medical devices remains a **draft** (the "finalized" headline in one outlet refers to the companion PCCP document), and the researchers did not confirm its status on fda.gov directly — label it draft ([MedDevice Guide](https://meddeviceguide.com/blog/fda-ai-enabled-device-software-functions-tplc-draft-guidance-guide)).

## Only the EU makes frontier-model evaluation a legal duty

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) contains the world's first legally binding model-evaluation mandate: **Article 55** requires providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk to "perform model evaluation in accordance with standardised protocols and tools reflecting the state of the art, including conducting and documenting adversarial testing" ([AI Act Explorer, Art. 55](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/55/)), applicable since 2 August 2025 and Commission-enforceable since **2 August 2026**, with fines up to EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover ([Commission FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/navigating-ai-act)). The product-safety half of the Act, by contrast, slipped: the **"Digital Omnibus" amendment** (in force 27 July 2026) pushed Annex III high-risk obligations to **2 December 2027** and Annex I embedded-AI obligations to **2 August 2028**, chiefly because the harmonised standards needed for conformity assessment were not ready ([Commission FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/navigating-ai-act); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). The amending regulation's reported number, (EU) 2026/1744, is **single-source and unverified against EUR-Lex**. The delay is itself a teaching point: evaluation infrastructure, not legal text, proved the rate-limiting resource.

The operative rulebook for frontier evaluation is the **GPAI Code of Practice** (published 10 July 2025), the voluntary compliance route the Commission and AI Board assessed as adequate ([Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/gpai-code-practice)). Its Safety and Security chapter — applying only to systemic-risk model providers — is the most detailed official specification of frontier evaluation practice anywhere: 10 commitments and 32 measures covering "cutting-edge" multi-modality evaluations (benchmarks, adversarial testing, human uplift studies, simulations), validity and reproducibility requirements, **full capability elicitation with anti-sandbagging provisions**, evaluator qualification floors (relevant PhD or 3 years' experience; at least 20 business days per evaluation task), **mandatory independent external evaluators before deployment** with publication rights after 30 business days and anti-retaliation protections, quantified risk-acceptance tiers, model reports to the AI Office, and severity-tiered incident reporting ([AI Act Explorer, Code of Practice overview](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/code-of-practice-overview/) — commitment detail is from this reliable secondary source; spot-check the [official chapter PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/118119) before quoting exact wording). Signatories include Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI, and OpenAI; **xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter, and Meta is absent from the list** (an absence, not a sourced refusal) ([Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/gpai-code-practice)). Institutionally, the **Scientific Panel of Independent Experts** — 60 members announced mid-2026, chaired by Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh — advises on evaluation methodologies and can alert the AI Office to systemic risks, making it the EU's answer to "who checks the evaluators" ([Commission, AI Scientific Panel](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/ai-scientific-panel)).

The standards pipeline that will eventually carry high-risk conformity assessment is behind schedule. Exactly **one harmonised standard** has been cited in the Official Journal: **EN 18286** on AI quality management systems (published 22 July 2026, cited 14 October 2026) ([jtc21.eu](https://jtc21.eu/)). The evaluation-critical drafts — risk management (prEN 18228), accuracy and robustness (prEN 18229-4/-5), bias (prEN 18283), the conformity assessment framework (prEN 18285) — remain in the pipeline, with several **reportedly rejected at public enquiry; these vote outcomes come from an unofficial tracker and cannot be verified against primary sources** ([KLA Digital tracker](https://kla.digital/blog/jtc-21-standards-tracker)). When the regime does arrive, most Annex III systems will be *self-assessed* against Annex IV technical documentation (test procedures, logs, reports, accuracy/robustness metrics), with third-party notified-body assessment mandatory mainly for biometrics ([AI Act Explorer, Art. 43](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/43/)) — so EU "conformity assessment" mostly produces documented self-evaluation, not independent testing. The sandbox infrastructure meant to generate pre-market real-world evidence is **largely prospective**: the Member State sandbox obligation slipped to 2 August 2027, and as of an August 2025 assessment only Spain had a working AI sandbox ([Dr. Datenschutz, citing EPRS](https://www.dr-datenschutz.de/regulatory-sandboxes-der-ki-verordnung-stand-huerden/)); the four EU Testing and Experimentation Facilities' specifics were **not verified this session**.

## The UK built the tools and the market, not the mandate

The UK regulates almost nothing but has produced the most concrete government-built evaluation artifacts anywhere. The **AI Security Institute** (renamed from "AI Safety Institute" in February 2025) publishes **Inspect**, an open-source Python framework for LLM evaluations co-developed with Meridian Labs — datasets/solvers/scorers architecture, a built-in ReAct agent, sandboxed code execution across Docker/Kubernetes/Modal, and 200+ prebuilt evaluations ([Inspect documentation](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/); license reported as MIT in package metadata — verify at the repo). Its methodological statement, **"Early lessons from evaluating frontier AI systems"** (October 2024), is arguably the single most teaching-rich government document on evaluation practice: evaluations are not certification (the science is "too nascent"); they should be *predictive*, using partial scoring to gauge distance from dangerous thresholds; elicitation tasks must be separated from test tasks to avoid inflated results; quality assurance rests on **preregistration** of methods before model access; and effective testing requires "Helpful Only" model variants and adequate testing windows ([AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/early-lessons-from-evaluating-frontier-ai-systems)). AISI's joint pre-deployment evaluations with the US institute — Claude 3.5 Sonnet (finding safeguards "can be routinely circumvented") and OpenAI's o1 — are the published worked examples ([AISI](https://aisi.gov.uk/work/pre-deployment-evaluation-of-anthropics-upgraded-claude-3-5-sonnet)); no public per-model report newer than o1 was found, with per-model findings apparently folded into the **Frontier AI Trends Report** (first edition 18 December 2025), which synthesizes two years of government testing ([GOV.UK factsheet](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet)); the "more than 30 systems tested" figure is **secondary (techUK), not confirmed in the official factsheet**.

DSIT's assurance programme is deliberately market-based: the **Portfolio of AI Assurance Techniques** defines the UK's de facto official taxonomy of **eight assurance technique types** — impact assessment, impact evaluation, bias audit, compliance audit, certification, conformity assessment, performance testing, formal verification ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/portfolio-of-ai-assurance-techniques)); **AI Management Essentials (AIME)** offers organisational self-assessment (still at consultation-outcome stage as of the February 2026 government response; **final tool status unverified**) ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/ai-management-essentials-tool)); and the **Trusted third-party AI assurance roadmap** (September 2025) plans a professionalised assurer market, naming four barriers (quality, skills, information access, innovation) and an **£11 million AI Assurance Innovation Fund** ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap)). For the public sector, the **Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard** is mandatory for departments and public-facing arm's-length bodies, producing a searchable public repository of records ([GOV.UK ATRS hub](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub)) — an evidence-publication requirement rather than a testing framework (the record template's fields were **not read**; don't describe them without checking). Sector regulators add the concrete edges: the ICO's Guidance on AI and data protection carries the UK's most specific regulator content on statistical accuracy and fairness measurement, currently flagged "**under review**" following the Data (Use and Access) Act ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/)), and the MHRA's **AI Airlock** sandbox for AI medical devices published a Phase 2 programme report finding that LLM-based devices "may begin to exhibit functions beyond their intended purpose when they lack active guardrails" ([Phase 2 report PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a676af523e57bd0a0ea6852/AI_Airlock_Phase_2_Programme_Report_v2.pdf); report dates come from **search metadata, not a fetched GOV.UK landing page**).

## Asia-Pacific spans the full spectrum from voluntary ecosystem to mandatory gate

The four Asian jurisdictions bracket every governance posture, which makes them the Atlas's best comparative set. **Singapore** has the most operationally complete voluntary stack: **AI Verify** tests organisational implementation against 11 governance principles through process checks plus technical testing, open-sourced since June 2023 and extended to generative AI in May 2025, with official crosswalks to the NIST AI RMF, the Hiroshima Code of Conduct, and ISO/IEC 42001 ([AI Verify Testing Framework](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/tools/ai-verify-testing-framework/)); **Project Moonshot** supplies the LLM benchmarking and red-teaming toolkit ([Project Moonshot](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/tools/moonshot/)); voluntary **Starter Kits** define test methodology for five LLM risk areas (v1.0 January 2026 — **date read from search results of the official PDF**) and, since 14 October 2026, for agentic systems with tool-use and handoff-accuracy metrics (**details from a law-firm note, secondary** — [Allen & Gledhill](https://www.allenandgledhill.com/sg/publication/articles/34742/imda-introduces-new-ai-testing-and-cybersecurity-initiatives)); the **Global AI Assurance Sandbox** pairs deployers with testing firms (30 applications across 12 geographies, per the official page) ([Sandbox](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/ai-assurance/sandbox/)); and the **AI TAP** programme accredits third-party testers (five firms accredited, secondary). **Japan** is the clearest case of an AI Safety Institute as national methodology publisher: its **Guide to Evaluation Perspectives on AI Safety** (v1.20, July 2026, Japanese; English confirmed only for v1.10) and **Guide to Red Teaming Methodology** (v1.10, March 2025) tell businesses *how* to evaluate ([J-AISI outputs](https://aisi.go.jp/output/)), joined in 2026 by healthcare and robotics sector guides — while the 2025 AI promotion Act imposes **no testing mandate at all** (penalty-free soft law; effective dates **conflict across sources** — [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/japans-first-ai-legislation-becomes-law-focus-promoting-research-and-development-no)). The guide's list of ten evaluation perspectives comes from a **third-party summary, unconfirmed against the official PDF**, and a reported v1.2 of the AI Guidelines for Business rests on a **single blog source — unverified**.

**South Korea** pairs Asia's first comprehensive binding AI law with mostly process obligations. The **AI Basic Act** (effective 22 January 2026) requires operators to self-assess whether their AI is "high-impact," document trust and safety measures, maintain human oversight, and make best efforts at fundamental-rights impact assessment, with "high-performance" AI above **10^26 cumulative training FLOPs** facing lifecycle risk-management and reporting duties — but **every statutory detail here comes from law-firm and news summaries; no official English translation was located** ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)). Fines are modest (up to ~US$21,000, limited violations), so the real evaluation capacity sits in the **Korea AI Safety Institute** (under ETRI since November 2024), reported to have evaluated **42 models by August 2026 with results disclosure practices conflicting across accounts** ([Money Today](https://www.mt.co.kr/en/tech/2026/08/07/2026080614255237204) — secondary; the institute's own site refused connections during research), and in TTA's nascent testing/certification market. **China** inverts the whole pattern: evaluation evidence exists to satisfy a **mandatory pre-launch gate**. Under the CAC's Interim Measures (effective 15 August 2023), generative AI services with "public opinion attributes or social mobilization capacity" must pass a security assessment and complete algorithm filing before launch (**article-level detail via legal summaries; the standard English translations were unreachable this session** — [Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/key-takeaways-from-chinas-finalized-generative-artificial-intelligence-measures/)). The concrete test methodology is **GB/T 45654-2025**, a recommended national standard whose appendices specify training-data and generated-content safety checks and assessment methods, consulted via **CSET's clearly labeled English translation** ([CSET](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/china-gen-ai-safety-standard-final/)); circulating numeric test parameters (refusal-rate targets, question-bank sizes) were **not verified and should not be cited**. Above it sits TC260's **AI Safety Governance Framework 2.0** (September 2025), adding five-level risk grading by scenario, intelligence, and scale (**via SESEC summary of the bilingual official document** — [SESEC](https://sesec.eu/?p=2036)).

## The international layer standardizes measurement, not verdicts

Canada and the international bodies show governance acting on evaluation *evidence* rather than running tests. **Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making** forces federal institutions to complete and publish an **Algorithmic Impact Assessment** before deploying an automated decision system: 65 risk questions and 41 mitigation questions yield a score mapped to four impact levels, which gate escalating peer-review, human-in-the-loop, and monitoring duties, with results published on the Open Government Portal ([canada.ca AIA page](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/automated-decision-making/algorithmic-impact-assessment.html) — the directive's own text **could not be fetched** due to site blocking; section-level claims rest on the official AIA page and secondary analysis). It is the longest-running operational example of impact-level-gated evaluation requirements. Canada's private-sector bill **AIDA is dead** — the AI minister said it will not be revived in its old form ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/?p=391578)) — leaving the Canadian AI Safety Institute ($50M over five years) as a research funder and joint-testing participant with **no published model-testing results of its own** ([ISED CAISI page](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/canadian-artificial-intelligence-safety-institute)).

The **International Network of AI Safety Institutes** — ten members including Australia, Canada, the EU, Japan, Kenya, Korea, Singapore, the UK, and the US — has run three joint testing exercises, which together form the closest thing to an intergovernmental reproducibility program for AI evaluation: a November 2024 pilot on Llama 3.1 405B whose headline lesson was that "small methodological differences and model optimization techniques can noticeably affect evaluation results" ([NIST fact sheet](https://www.nist.gov/node/1867351)); a 2025 multilingual exercise led by Singapore, Japan, and the UK ([Japan AISI report PDF](https://aisi.go.jp/assets/pdf/International-Network-of-AI-Safety-Institutes-Joint-Testing-Exercise-Improving-Methodologies-for-AI-Model-Evaluations-Across-Global-Languages.pdf)); and a 2026 agentic exercise covering sensitive-information leakage, fraud, and cybersecurity ([arXiv 2601.15679](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15679.pdf) — strand-leadership details from summaries, PDFs not fully read). In December 2025 the Network was renamed the **International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science**, dropping "safety" ([MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/amp/articles/2419745)); one tracker dates the rename February 2026, and **no official statement resolving the discrepancy was found**. The G7's **HAIP Reporting Framework** (OECD-hosted, live February 2025) is the canonical evaluation-*disclosure* mechanism: a voluntary questionnaire on the Hiroshima Code of Conduct's 11 actions, producing 25 public first-round reports whose OECD analysis found widespread red-teaming and growing use of AI to test AI ([HAIP about page](https://oecd.ai/en/transparency/about); [OECD wonk](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/g7-haip-report-insights-for-ai-governance-and-risk-management)). The OECD also brackets the lifecycle with its **AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor** (LLM classification of ~1,000 news events daily yielding ~10 incidents/hazards) ([methodology](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents-methodology)) and the **beta** AI Capability Indicators, a nine-domain, five-level scale for policymakers ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/introducing-the-oecd-ai-capability-indicators_be745f04-en.html)); whether either has moved past beta/in-development by November 2026 is **unconfirmed**.

Two more layers complete the picture. The **International AI Safety Report** (Bengio-chaired, 100+ experts, 30+ countries, UK AISI secretariat; 2026 edition 3 February 2026) is the field's most authoritative statement that evaluation science is immature — the "evaluation gap" between pre-deployment tests and real-world behaviour, no internationally agreed methodology, and models exhibiting "evaluation awareness" — though these characterizations come from **secondary summaries; verify against the [2026 PDF](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026_1.pdf) before quoting** ([internationalaisafetyreport.org](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/)). And ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 is building the formal standards stack: **ISO/IEC TS 42119-2:2025** (overview of AI testing, published November 2025) atop the 2020 TR 29119-11 guidelines, with parts on V&V analysis (42119-3), **red teaming (42119-7)**, and GenAI quality assessment (42119-8) all **under development** ([EVS](https://www.evs.ee/en/iso-iec-ts-42119-2-2025); [ISO 42119-7 project page](https://www.iso.org/standard/91240.html); series overview from a **vendor source** — [SGS](https://www.sgs.com/en-au/news/2026/01/announcing-the-iso-iec-42119-series-a-new-era-for-ai-testing-and-assurance)), alongside the 24029 robustness series, the 25059 AI quality model, and **ISO/IEC 42006:2025**, which standardizes the auditors who certify AI management systems ([iso.org](https://www.iso.org/standard/44546.html)). Briefer national entries round out the map: Australia's ten mandatory guardrails remain **proposed, not enacted, and de-emphasized** by the December 2025 National AI Plan ([Montreal AI Ethics Institute](https://montrealethics.ai/ai-policy-corner-from-proposed-mandatory-guardrails-to-the-national-ai-plan-ai-governance-in-australia/) — no primary confirmation of formal abandonment); Germany's BSI **AIC4** catalogue (2021) sets auditable lifecycle criteria for AI cloud services ([BSI](https://www.bsi.bund.de/EN/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Informationen-und-Empfehlungen/Kuenstliche-Intelligenz/AIC4/aic4.html)); France's **LNE** runs voluntary certification of AI processes — design, development, evaluation, maintenance — certifying processes, not products ([LNE](https://www.lne.fr/en/service/certification/certification-processes-ai)).

## Conclusion

The deepest pattern across all five research strands is that the same technical activities — benchmarks, red teaming, field testing, incident logging — serve radically different institutional functions depending on jurisdiction: a licensing-like gate in China, a legal duty with external-evaluator rights in the EU, voluntary assurance evidence in Singapore and the UK, federal procurement hygiene in the US and Canada, and cross-lab measurement science in the international network. An Atlas organized around *methods* can therefore use governments as its worked examples of *purpose*: what changes between regimes is not how you run an evaluation but who demands the evidence, who is allowed to see it, and what happens when a system fails. A second, humbling pattern is that the most instructive documents are the least settled: TEVV-Athlon is a draft, ARIA stopped at a pilot, the EU's testing standards keep failing enquiry votes, Korea's law is readable only through law-firm summaries, and the Bengio report's verdict is that nobody's methodology is validated. The Atlas's own no-fabrication and visible-draft-status rules are not just editorial hygiene here — they mirror the field's actual epistemic state, where the honest label on most government evaluation frameworks in November 2026 is still "in progress."
